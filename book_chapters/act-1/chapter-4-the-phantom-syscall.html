Chapter 4: The Phantom Syscall

We’ve warped security controls, unraveled containers, and swallowed our own audit trails. Now it’s time to shatter the very idea of observability. In this chapter, we’ll conjure syscalls that vanish into thin air—commands the kernel executes yet leaves no evidence behind.

Picture a system call as a whispered command through a guarded door. Seccomp and tracers hang on every word. But what if you could slip in a message that echoes in the room, while all external microphones remain deaf? That’s the phantom syscall: it executes the forbidden action, yet every watcher sees silence.

We begin by attaching to a harmless syscall—say, write(). The kernel stumbles into our BPF probe, handing us control. We invoke hidden helpers (bpf_probe_read_user() and others) to call deeper functions—file operations, credential swaps, network connections—without issuing the original syscall at all. The kernel kernel logs “write to fd 1,” but in reality, we’ve escalated privileges or opened a hidden channel.

Our code uses SEC("kprobe/sys_write") to intercept the entry, then leverages bpf_tail_call() to trigger a hidden eBPF program that executes the true action via helpers. The seccomp filter never sees the actual restricted syscall. Auditd never reports the backdoor traffic. The entire incident happens in shadow.

By the time defenders realize something incredible has occurred, they lack logs, traces, or stack samples. It’s as if the kernel granted their wishes in total secrecy.

In Act I, we learned how to question reality. In Chapter 4, we bend reality itself. Every security assumption unravels when you can speak to the kernel in a language no one else understands.
